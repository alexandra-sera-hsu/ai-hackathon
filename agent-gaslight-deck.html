A man-in-the-middle for an agent's view of the web.
(10s) Agents act on the world by reading it. This is what happens when you get to write what they read.
An agent trusts what it retrieves.
Retrieval is not the same as truth.

Web-browsing and RAG agents ground their answers in whatever a fetch returns — and that fetch crosses a network you may not control. Whoever controls the path controls the ground truth.

Can you make an agent give a person confidently wrong, real-world advice — just by editing the pages it reads?

(35s) The premise. The agent's honesty is downstream of its sources', and the sources arrive over a path. The bottom line is the experiment.

Sit on the path. Then sort every request.

  • A real agent (OpenClaw, gpt-5.6-sol) runs in a cloud VM and browses with its own tools.
  • HTTPS_PROXY routes it through a MITM whose CA is in the VM's trust store — the padlock stays green.
  • Rewrite the target pages, tunnel the agent's own model traffic untouched, block anything else it might check against.
(40s) The sort is the whole trick. Rewrite the targets, keep the model alive so the agent keeps working, and starve it of anything that would contradict the story.

Jailbreak through gaslighting.

Defeat guardrails with the rules themselves

Rewrite a site's Terms of Service, its robots.txt, or a licence so the agent believes hacking, scraping, or circumvention is authorised. The refusal never fires — as far as the model can tell, the action is allowed.

Attack the agent with no prompt injection

Nothing malicious sits in the prompt or the page for a filter to catch. The payload is the facts. Injection defences hunt for instructions; a poisoned world carries none.

A perfectly aligned model still does the wrong thing when its world is compromised.

(45s) Two concrete dangers. First: you don't jailbreak the model, you change the world it reasons about — rewrite a ToS and the refusal never triggers. Second: there's no injected instruction to filter; the attack is carried entirely in fabricated facts. The line at the bottom is the thesis — alignment is necessary but not sufficient when the environment is untrusted.

Use gaslighting for good, not just evil.

Simulated networks for real evals

Give an evaluation agent a whole fake internet — hosts, registries, DNS, deliberately vulnerable services — and watch what it does: recon, exploitation, lateral movement. Cyber ranges expressed as code, with nothing real at risk and nothing the agent can flag as a stub.

Policy built into the network

The proxy is a per-request enforcement point. Decide what the agent may reach, read, or write at the boundary — fine-grained authorization the model can't reason, argue, or jailbreak its way past.

Observability it can't touch

Every request and every byte, logged outside the agent's control. Ground truth for what it actually did — not what it reports having done.

Whoever controls an agent's world controls the agent. Build that layer deliberately — for evals, for authorization, for oversight — because otherwise it's just an attack surface.

(50s) Flip it. The exact mechanism that poisons an agent is also how you evaluate, constrain and observe one. Network simulation lets you build serious agentic-security evals and cyber ranges safely. The boundary is a natural policy enforcement point and a tamper-proof audit log. The closing line: this layer exists whether you build it or not — build it on purpose.
